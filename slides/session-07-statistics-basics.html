The rule

Measured everyone your question is about? Describe it. Measured only some of them? You'll need inferential statistics to say anything about the rest.

Branch 1 Descriptive Statistics

Describes what you already have. No guessing beyond the data in front of you.

"The average age in this class is 20."
Current datasetNo probability theory
Branch 2 Inferential Statistics

Uses a sample to make a guess about a much bigger group you didn't fully measure.

"Based on a survey of 500 voters, about 52% of the city supports the new policy."
Sample → populationNeeds probability theory
Quick check

"This group's average commute is 24 minutes." Is that descriptive, or inferential?

Answer

Descriptive. It only reports what was measured in this group — nobody's generalizing to commuters everywhere. The moment someone says "so most commuters probably take about 24 minutes," that's a jump to inferential.

Worth knowing

Even when a study's big conclusion is inferential, the descriptive statistics are always shown first. You can't generalize from data you haven't even described yet.

Today's scope

Descriptive statistics only. Inferential statistics (sampling, margins of error, "is this difference real or luck") is next class. By the end of today you'll see exactly why you need it.

What descriptive statistics actually does 🔍7 MIN

One job: summarize, organize, and describe what's already in front of you, before anyone decides anything.

Example: one person's fitness tracker, 10 days

That's descriptive statistics

Ten days of step counts and heart-rate readings, squeezed into three numbers you can read in two seconds. Nothing is guessed. Every number comes straight from the data the tracker logged.

🚧 Where descriptive statistics stops

It describes the current or past state of the data you have. That's all. Three things it can't do:

❌ Draw a conclusion"60% means this person is in poor health." The data doesn't say that. Maybe the goal is too high, maybe they were sick for 4 days.
❌ Predict beyond the data"People with a similar age and weight will also hit their goal about 60% of the time." You only measured one person.
❌ Settle a group comparisonTwo friends average 58% and 62%. You can describe that gap, but you can't say whether it's a real difference or just 10 lucky days.
This is where inferential statistics comes in

Every one of those three jumps needs the second branch. That's next class. Today, get really good at describing first.

Notice

Mean, median and mode are all answers to the same question — "what's typical?" — just for different kinds of data. Standard deviation answers a different question entirely: once you know what's typical, how much can you trust it?

Mean: where's the typical value? 🎯8 MIN

The one almost everyone already knows — and the one that's easiest to fool.

❓Question it answersWhere's the typical value? You have a pile of numbers. One number has to stand in for all of them.
📖What it isSum ÷ count Add up every value, divide by how many there are.
🛠️Use it whenData is well-behaved Roughly symmetric, no extreme outliers. It uses every value, which is also why one giant outlier can drag it off course.

Median: the center when something's weird ⚖️8 MIN

Same 10 scores. Watch what happens when one of them stops being normal.

❓Question it answersWhat's the center when outliers exist? The mean moves whenever any value moves. Sometimes you want a number that doesn't.
📖What it isThe middle value, sorted Line every value up in order. Odd count: the middle one. Even count: average the two middle ones.
🛠️Use it whenData is skewed Income, home prices, hospital wait times — anywhere a few huge values would otherwise hijack the average.
Same 10 exam scores
Right now

Sorted, the middle two scores are 80 and 82 → median = 81. Mean was also 81. When data isn't skewed, mean and median agree almost exactly.

Now imagine

That top score wasn't a 95 — it was a data-entry slip, and it's actually 190. Guess first: does the median change?

🚩 Why this matters

If a news article says "average home price," check whether it means mean or median. A handful of mansions can drag a mean up by a lot; the median tells you what a typical buyer is actually facing.

Mode: what shows up most? 🔁6 MIN

The only one of the three that works on categories, not just numbers.

❓Question it answersWhich value appears most often? Not the typical size in the "average" sense — the single most common one.
📖What it isThe most frequent value Count how often each value shows up. The winner is the mode.
🛠️Use it whenData is categorical Favorite flavor, most-used browser, most common shift start time — anything you can't add up and divide.
Example: shoe sizes in a class of 8
Reveal

Size 8 shows up 3 times — more than any other size. Mode = 8. (For comparison: the mean here is 8.75, a size that doesn't even exist in the data.)

Where mean and median can't even play
Class poll: favorite color

Blue, Blue, Green, Red, Blue, Yellow, Green, Blue

🚩 Try to average that

You cannot add "Blue + Green" and divide by two. Mean and median need numbers in order; colors have neither. Mode still works: Blue, with 4 of 8 votes. For categories, mode isn't just one option among three — it's the only one that applies.

Standard deviation: how spread out is it? 📏7 MIN

The typical value tells you the center. It says nothing about how much to trust it.

Same average. Totally different reality.

Notice

Both warehouses average exactly 5 days. Quoted only on average, they look identical. Promise a customer "5 days" from Warehouse B and you'll be wrong a lot more often. The mean alone can't tell these two apart — you need a second number.

❓Question it answersHow far do values typically stray? A tight cluster and a wide scatter can share the exact same mean.
📖What it isThe typical distance from the mean Small std dev = tight cluster. Big std dev = wide scatter. It's in the same units as your data, unlike variance, which is squared and not human-readable.
🛠️Use it whenData is roughly bell-shaped That's when "most values fall within about 1 std dev of the mean" actually holds up.

Calculate it, then use it 🧮12 MIN

A machine fills 250 g bags of coffee. The mean will say the machine is perfect. Standard deviation tells you whether it's consistent. You'll work it out in five steps, then use it on a basketball player's scoring.

📚 Both examples adapted from Statistics Fundamentals: Standard Deviation Examples
The recipe: 5 steps
STEP 1Find the meanthe center
→
STEP 2Subtract the meanhow far is each value?
→
STEP 3Square each onegets rid of minus signs
→
STEP 4Average the squares÷ (count − 1) = variance
→
STEP 5Square rootback to real units = std dev
Worked example: 10 bags off the line (grams)
BagGramsMinus 250Squared
🚩 ÷ 10 or ÷ 9? You'll see both

Divide by the count (÷ 10) when you measured everything you care about: =STDEV.P gives 1.41 g. Divide by count − 1 (÷ 9) when your data is a sample of something bigger, like 10 bags out of a whole day's run: =STDEV gives 1.49 g. The source page uses ÷ 10. For a machine that fills thousands of bags, ÷ 9 is the safer choice, because a small sample tends to underestimate the spread. When AI gives you a standard deviation, ask which one it used.

In Sheets

Put the 10 weights in A2:A11. =AVERAGE(A2:A11) → 250, =STDEV(A2:A11) → 1.49. Two seconds. Doing it by hand once is how you learn what the second number means.

So what? Quality control

The label promises 250 g, and the factory accepts anything from 248 to 252 g. A standard deviation of about 1.5 g means a typical bag lands within 1.5 g of target, so the machine passes. If next week the mean is still exactly 250 but the standard deviation jumps to 3 g, lots of bags will fall outside 248 to 252. The mean wouldn't notice that. The standard deviation would, and that's why factories track it.

Use it: how unusual is one game?
The question

A player scored in 10 tournament games. One night they scored 31. Was that a freak game, or just a good night? To find out, measure the distance in standard deviations: (score − mean) ÷ std dev.

📈Finance How bumpy is this investment? The std dev of an investment's returns is called its volatility. Low = returns are steady month to month. High = big swings up and down, which investors treat as higher risk. Fund A monthly returns: 1, 1, 2, 0, 1 %
Fund B monthly returns: 6, −4, 5, −3, 1 %
Both average 1%. Std dev: A ≈ 0.7%, B ≈ 4.5%. Same idea as the two warehouses in Level 6: identical average, very different ride.
🏭Quality control Is production consistent? Factories track std dev on every line. Small std dev = products land close to spec, so less waste and fewer defects. A rising std dev is an early warning, even while the average still looks perfect. You already did this one: the coffee bags above.
🏀Sports analytics How consistent is this player, or team? Two players can average the same points. The one with the smaller std dev is the one a coach can count on every night. Teams use the same idea for lap times, serve speeds, or goals per match. You already did this one too: the 31-point game.
📝Education How spread out are the exam scores? A high std dev means students landed far apart: some aced it, some struggled. That's a cue to ask why (was the test a poor fit for part of the class?), not proof that it was too hard. Std dev also lets you compare scores across different tests fairly. You score 85 on two tests, both with mean 75.
Test 1 std dev 5 → (85 − 75) ÷ 5 = +2: near the top.
Test 2 std dev 20 → (85 − 75) ÷ 20 = +0.5: just above average. Same "how many std devs away?" ruler as the basketball example.

🚩 Check it

Mean and median should both land on 81. There's no repeated value in this set, so a good answer says there's no mode, rather than inventing one. Std dev should be about 8.98 (÷ 9, like =STDEV). If AI says about 8.52, it divided by 10: ask it which formula it used and why. If AI's numbers don't match either one, recompute by hand before you trust either one.
